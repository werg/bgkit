\begin{abstract}
\bgkit{} gives a language model access to far more text than fits in its context budget. A
bidirectional encoder reads a text together with a prompt that states what the reader needs, compresses
it by a chosen ratio, and projects the result into the decoder's input embedding space, where the
decoder reads it in place of the text's tokens. Any number of such compressed slots can be mixed with
ordinary text, and the same interface carries documents, tool results, conversation histories and
repositories. We train a 350M-parameter encoder with a 350M-parameter decoder on a single workstation
GPU and evaluate at a fixed number of decoder positions on nine long-context benchmarks, against
truncated text, BM25 retrieval, LongLLMLingua and question-focused summaries written by a 35B model.
With 2{,}048 positions the decoder answers questions over 59k-token stories and 111k-token
conversation histories. The compressed context scores significantly above truncated text in 16 of 26
settings and above LongLLMLingua in 13 of 22, and is never significantly below either outside RULER.
Where an answer combines facts spread through the context (BABILong, HotpotQA, MuSiQue) it is also
above BM25, and in 512 positions it scores higher on BABILong than any text method given 2{,}048
(0.79 against at most 0.38). With the same decoder weights, the compressed context also scores above
the entire untruncated text on BABILong, HotpotQA and LoCoMo (for example 0.64 against 0.36 on
HotpotQA in about a fifth of the positions): on these tasks, reading through the encoder adds accuracy, not
only reach. The gain comes from the encoder's vectors, not only
from which positions it keeps: at the same kept positions, the tokens themselves score far lower
(0.26 against 0.74 on BABILong 16k), and an encoding made without the question loses most of its
value. Exact strings are not preserved: on RULER's needle tasks, BM25 and summaries score higher. Over a
110k-token context the decoder needs 0.1~GB instead of 5.0~GB of memory beyond its weights and decodes
four times faster per token; the encoder pass costs about as much compute as a full-context prefill. An
agent that receives every tool result in compressed form runs SWE-bench Lite localisation and
web-research episodes end to end with fewer decoder tokens than when it reads text.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A language model can use only what fits in its context window. Retrieved documents, tool output,
earlier conversation and source code compete for the same positions, and every position costs a full
pass through the model. Vision--language models handle a similar problem for images: a separate
encoder turns many pixels into a few vectors, which are projected into the language model's
token-embedding space and read like tokens \citep{alayrac2022flamingo,liu2023llava}.

\bgkit{} applies this to text. An encoder reads a text together with a prompt that states what the
reader needs from it, for example \emph{``answer: who founded the company?''} or \emph{``lines 80--140
of \texttt{io.py}: where is the buffer flushed?''}, and emits a short sequence of vectors in the
decoder's input embedding space. The decoder reads them in a slot in place of the text
(Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). The compression ratio is chosen per slot at inference time, and an input can
hold any number of slots alongside ordinary text. Because the slots are vectors in the decoder's own
embedding space, the encoder can also read its own output, which lets it summarise directory trees and
long histories hierarchically. An encoding made under a question-independent prompt can also stay in
context: a standing, compressed view of a repository, a memory or a long history, available to the
model without spending its context window on the raw text. \bgkit{} is meant as a building block for
this kind of ambient awareness beyond the context window.

The paper makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A method} (Sections~\ref{sec:method}--\ref{sec:training}): the encoder, projection and
  slot interface, the training recipe, and an agent harness in which every tool result arrives
  compressed.
\item \textbf{Measurements of context savings} (Sections~\ref{sec:sweep}--\ref{sec:cost}): nine
  long-context benchmarks at a fixed decoder budget, against four text-side ways of fitting a context
  into the same budget and against the original decoder reading the whole context; the budget a
  text method needs to match the compressed context; the effect of the compression ratio; and the
  end-to-end cost.
\item \textbf{What the compressed context carries} (Section~\ref{sec:vectors}): at the encoder's own
  kept positions, its vectors against the tokens at those positions, and encodings made with and
  without the question.
\end{itemize}
Section~\ref{sec:agents} runs an agent that reads only compressed context on SWE-bench Lite bug
localisation, web research and conversational memory.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Encoder.}
The encoder is LFM2.5-Encoder-350M, a 16-layer bidirectional model with hidden size 1024. Its input
is a prompt, a separator and the text to encode, so every text position can attend to the prompt.

\paragraph{Compression.}
After the middle layer, a scoring head ranks the text positions, and the top $k=\lceil rN\rceil$ of
the $N$ positions are kept for a compression ratio $r$. The remaining layers still run over the
whole sequence, so each kept position can gather information from anywhere in the text. The final
hidden states at the kept positions form the encoding. The ratio is a free parameter at inference
time: the same weights produce 4$\times$, 16$\times$ or 128$\times$ encodings.

\paragraph{Projection.}
A projection stage (an attention layer over the kept positions followed by an MLP) maps the encoding
into the decoder's input embedding space. A normalisation layer then matches the per-channel
statistics of the decoder's token embeddings. We added it after observing, in earlier versions, that
the projected vectors could collapse onto a shared direction while keeping a plausible norm.

\paragraph{Decoder.}
The decoder is LFM2.5-350M (instruction-tuned), and its input is rendered with its own chat template.
Each slot is marked by a placeholder token that is replaced by the slot's vectors, and an input may
contain any number of slots interleaved with text. A rank-32 low-rank adapter on the decoder's first
eight layers is trained to read the slots. The adapter applies at all positions, so it also changes
how the decoder reads ordinary text.

\paragraph{Hierarchical encodings.}
Because the vectors live in the decoder's embedding space, the encoder can also be applied to its
own output. We use this in three places:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Repositories} are encoded bottom-up. Each file is encoded at 8$\times$, and each directory
  encodes its children's vectors at a further 4$\times$, up to a root \emph{overview} of a few
  hundred vectors. Nodes are cached and can be opened with a \texttt{browse} tool.
\item \emph{Long histories} are compacted by folding older turns into cells encoded at 16$\times$.
  Cells merge pairwise at 4$\times$, so the compacted history grows roughly logarithmically.
\item \emph{Conversational memory} is a set of per-session encodings with a compact index of all
  sessions. A session can be re-read under a new prompt.
\end{itemize}
The evaluations below exercise the repository overview and the memory index. History compaction is
not evaluated separately.

\paragraph{Agent harness.}
In our agent harness, file reads, code search, shell output, web search, page reads, find-in-page and
memory lookups all return slots rather than text. Each slot is encoded under the agent's own
statement of what it is looking for. Tools have fixed compression ratios: 4$\times$ for search hits
and find-in-page, where answers are often copied verbatim; 8$\times$ for file reads; 16$\times$ for
other search, shell output and page reads; 32$\times$ for the repository overview and compacted
history.

\section{Training}
\label{sec:training}

\paragraph{Objectives.}
The decoder is trained to produce a target (an answer, an agent's next turn, or a continuation of
the text) from an input in which the context appears only as encoded vectors. Three further signals
are added to this cross-entropy loss:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Distillation from a full-text reader.} A decoder with a separately trained adapter reads
  the same input with the context as plain text, and the model reading encodings is trained to match
  its next-token distribution.
\item \emph{Prompt contrast.} A document is also encoded under a different question about it, and the
  correct answer is trained to be more likely under the right prompt than under the wrong one.
\item \emph{Preservation.} On a stream of ordinary chat and long-text reading, the adapted decoder is
  distilled towards the original decoder, to limit damage to its general behaviour.
\end{itemize}
The scoring head receives a small auxiliary loss that pushes it to follow the full-text reader's
attention from the answer to the context.

\paragraph{Compression curriculum.}
Each example's ratio is sampled between 4$\times$ and a floor that moves from 64$\times$ towards
128$\times$ during training. In agent data, tool slots use the harness ratios, randomly scaled by up
to a factor of two.

\paragraph{Data.}
Training mixes reconstruction and continuation of plain text (web text, code, books, dialogue) with
task data rendered as conversations whose contexts become slots:
\begin{itemize}
\item reading comprehension and long-document QA, including the training splits of HotpotQA, QuALITY
  and NarrativeQA (benchmark-test stories excluded);
\item bAbI tasks 1--3 (training split) hidden in book, web and wiki filler, which is how BABILong
  builds its test set;
\item multi-hop QA with distractors and synthetic needle retrieval;
\item agent trajectories on software repositories (generated by a larger teacher model and
  rejection-sampled), with repository QA, file localisation and repository overviews;
\item web-research trajectories mapped onto the web tools;
\item multi-session conversations (from MSC, Chronicles and SHARE) with generated questions about
  their history.
\end{itemize}
No evaluation item appears in training. BABILong, HotpotQA, QuALITY and NarrativeQA are therefore
in-distribution task formats; MuSiQue, RULER, LongMemEval and LoCoMo were used only for evaluation.

\paragraph{Parameters and compute.}
In the final stages the base encoder weights are frozen. A rank-16 adapter on its top four layers,
the scoring head and the projection are trained, together with the decoder adapter. All training ran
on a single NVIDIA DGX Spark. The evaluated checkpoint ends a chain of warm-started development runs:
its final three stages took 40{,}000 steps (8{,}000, 12{,}000 and 20{,}000), with the agentic, web
and memory data at full weight in the last 20{,}000. The encoder and adapters inherit weights from
earlier runs on overlapping data.

\section{Evaluation setup}
\label{sec:setup}

Every benchmark comparison uses a fixed \emph{budget}: the number of decoder positions the context may
occupy. The arms are:
\begin{description}
\item[bgkit] The context is compressed to fit the budget, with the question as the encoder prompt.
\item[text] The same trained decoder reads the context as text, middle-truncated to the budget.
\item[base] The original decoder (adapter off) reads the same truncated text.
\item[BM25] Retrieval at the budget: the context is cut into passages of at most 128 tokens, the
  passages are ranked against the question with BM25, and the best ones are kept up to the budget,
  in document order.
\item[LongLLMLingua] Question-aware token pruning \citep{jiang2024longllmlingua} to the budget, with
  the method's recommended settings and Qwen3-1.7B as the scoring model (five times the size of our
  encoder); the result is cut to the budget in the decoder's tokens.
\item[summary] Qwen3.6-35B-A3B writes a question-focused summary at the budget (map-reduce over
  12k-token chunks for longer contexts).
\item[base, full context] The original decoder reads the whole context without truncation. This arm
  is not budget-matched.
\end{description}
The trained decoder was trained to read compressed slots, not to answer from text, so it may read
text worse than the original decoder does. The three question-aware text baselines (BM25,
LongLLMLingua, summary) were therefore read by both decoders, and each cell reports the better of
the two; the \emph{text} and \emph{base} columns report truncated text under each decoder.

BABILong \citep{kuratov2024babilong} (tasks qa1--qa3), RULER \citep{hsieh2024ruler} and QuALITY
\citep{pang2022quality} are scored by accuracy. NarrativeQA \citep{kocisky2018narrativeqa}, HotpotQA
\citep{yang2018hotpotqa}, MuSiQue \citep{trivedi2022musique}, LongMemEval \citep{wu2025longmemeval}
and LoCoMo \citep{maharana2024locomo} are scored by an LLM judge (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B with
LongMemEval's yes/no judging prompt; a 4-bit GGUF build for the arms of the original sweep and an NVFP4
build for the baselines added later; an FP8 build agreed with the original on 99\% of 1{,}500 sweep
verdicts). All arms are scored on the same items. Cells have 60--100 items;
we compare arms by the mean per-item difference with a 95\% paired-bootstrap interval (10{,}000
resamples), given for every cell in Appendix~\ref{app:stats}, and call a difference significant when
the interval excludes zero.

\paragraph{Benchmarks not used in training.}
MuSiQue, RULER, LongMemEval and LoCoMo were used only for evaluation. On MuSiQue the compressed
context scores 0.30--0.33, against 0.08--0.16 for truncated text, 0.17--0.19 for BM25 and 0.13 for the
original decoder reading the entire context. On LoCoMo it scores 0.28, against 0.09--0.16 for truncated
text and 0.19 for the entire context, and on LongMemEval-S (111k tokens per history) 0.20--0.30 against
0.15--0.18 for truncated text. On these two memory benchmarks BM25 retrieval scores about as high
(0.27--0.34; no difference is significant). RULER is the exception, discussed below.

\paragraph{Against text baselines at the same budget.}
Appendix~\ref{app:stats} gives the paired difference for every cell. The compressed context scores
significantly above truncated text in 16 of 26 cells and above LongLLMLingua in 13 of 22, and is
significantly below either only on RULER (LongLLMLingua at 2{,}048 positions). Against BM25 it is
significantly above on BABILong (by 0.29--0.50), HotpotQA (0.14--0.25) and MuSiQue (0.11--0.15),
within noise on QuALITY, NarrativeQA, LongMemEval-S and LoCoMo, and below on RULER. The questions
where it leads are those whose answer combines facts spread through the context: bAbI's chains of
movements, and multi-hop questions over several paragraphs. Question-focused summaries written by the
35B model, whose generation costs far more than one pass of the 350M encoder, score higher on MuSiQue
(0.43 against 0.30--0.31, significant at 512 positions) and on RULER, about as high on HotpotQA and
QuALITY, and lower on BABILong 4k (0.25--0.32 against 0.79--0.81).

\paragraph{Against the whole context.}
Table~\ref{tab:full} compares the compressed context with the whole, untruncated text, read once by the

With the same weights, the compressed context scores significantly higher on BABILong (0.74--0.79

\paragraph{Exact strings.}
RULER's needle tasks ask for exact keys and numbers. The full context scores 0.58, BM25 0.42--0.54
and the compressed context 0.17--0.20: arbitrary strings are not preserved at these ratios, and a
method that returns the matching passage verbatim does better. The agent harness accordingly reads
search hits at only 4$\times$.

\section{Cost}
\label{sec:cost}

Table~\ref{tab:cost} measures one question over a long context with the released model (bf16, one
GPU, medians of three runs after a warm-up): the original decoder prefilling the whole context, against
the encoder pass over the context in 2{,}048-token chunks followed by a decoder prefill over the slots.
The decoder's own cost falls with the number of positions it reads. At 110k tokens, the memory the
decoder needs beyond its weights drops from 5.0~GB to 0.1~GB, and each generated token takes 12--13~ms
instead of 53~ms. The encoder pass is not free: with an encoder as large as the decoder, its forward
FLOPs over the context about equal those of the full-context prefill, so time to the first token is
similar up to 64k tokens and lower at 110k (5.3--5.7~s against 8.9~s), where the decoder's attention
over the full context grows faster than the chunked encoder. The encoder's cost does not depend on the
decoder, so with a larger decoder the prefill saving would grow in proportion to its size; we did not
measure that pairing. An encoding is specific to its question (Section~\ref{sec:vectors}), so each new
question needs a new encoder pass, whereas a full-context prefill can be cached and reused across
questions.

\section{What the compressed context carries}
\label{sec:vectors}

The encoder does two things: it chooses which positions to keep, and it computes a vector at each kept
position after the remaining layers have attended over the whole text and the prompt. Two
measurements separate these.

\paragraph{Vectors against the tokens they sit on.}
In the \emph{kept tokens} arm the encoder runs exactly as in the \bgkit{} arm, with the same question
as prompt and the same budget, but the decoder receives the token embeddings at the kept positions,
in document order, instead of the encoder's vectors (Table~\ref{tab:format}). The positions and
their number are identical; only what occupies them differs. The vectors score significantly higher
on seven of nine benchmarks: 0.74 against 0.26 on BABILong 16k, 0.64 against 0.31 on HotpotQA, 0.30
against 0.07 on LongMemEval-S; QuALITY and NarrativeQA are within noise. The kept tokens on their
own score in the range of LongLLMLingua's question-aware token pruning at the same budget (higher on
BABILong, lower on the two memory benchmarks). Most of the
compressed context's advantage therefore lies in the vectors: a vector at a kept position carries
information from text that was not kept, which a selection of tokens cannot.

\paragraph{The prompt and the selection.}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} varies how the encoding is produced, on the items and checkpoint of
Table~\ref{tab:ratio}. Encoding without the question lowers exact match from 0.58--0.90 to 0.04--0.21.

bAbI-in-filler (0.82 against 0.90). What the vectors carry thus depends mainly on the prompt they were
computed under, and less on which positions hold them. The encoding is specific to its prompt: one made
under one question can be reused for another only when the prompt does not depend on the question, as
for the repository overview and the memory index.

\section{Agentic tasks}
\label{sec:agents}

Agent evaluations run the full harness with at most 12 turns and 40{,}000 decoder tokens per episode.
In the \emph{text} arms, tool results are returned as text cut to the same per-result budget. The
policy was trained on episodes with compressed tool results, so the text arms run it outside its
training distribution.

\paragraph{Bug localisation.}
On SWE-bench Lite \citep{jimenez2024swebench} the agent starts from the issue and the repository at
its base commit, explores, and names the file to change. We excluded the 114 Django instances, whose
repository trees exceeded our caching limit, leaving 186 (Table~\ref{tab:swe}). With the repository
overview, a few hundred vectors standing for the whole tree, in its context, the agent commits to an
answer in 59\% of episodes instead of 22\%, never ends in a loop (70\% without it), and uses 13.9k
instead of 19.5k decoder tokens. It names a file edited by the reference patch in 14 of 186 episodes
(7.5\%; 95\% interval about 4.5--12\%), against 1 for the text agent with the same overview. The
agent made no \texttt{browse} calls, so this measures the root overview alone.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Baselines.} No decoder was trained on the same data with text contexts at the same
  budget; text baselines are read by the slot-trained decoder or the original one, whichever scores
  higher. An adapter from an earlier, shorter run (4{,}000 steps on a different mix of reading and
  agent data) trained to read text placed in the slot scores significantly below the compressed
  context in 20 of 26 cells and above it in none, but it is not a matched baseline. LongLLMLingua used
  a 1.7B scoring model, and was run at one budget on the two longest benchmarks.
\item \textbf{Task overlap.} The task formats of BABILong, HotpotQA, QuALITY and NarrativeQA are in
  training (their training splits, or bAbI's generator for BABILong). MuSiQue, RULER, LongMemEval and
  LoCoMo were not trained on.
\item \textbf{Scale and sample size.} Encoder and decoder have 350M parameters each, evaluation cells
  have 60--186 items, and the agentic results rest on small counts. We ran one seed.
\item \textbf{Encoder cost.} An encoding made under a question serves that question, so each new
  question needs a new encoder pass over the text. With equal-sized encoder and decoder, that pass
  costs about as much compute as a full-context prefill (Section~\ref{sec:cost}); the savings are in
  decoder positions, memory and per-token decoding time.
\item \textbf{Exact strings.} Arbitrary keys and numbers are lost at these ratios (RULER).
\item \textbf{Training history.} The evaluated model ends a chain of warm-started development runs,
  and the recipe has not been reproduced from scratch.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

Prior work compresses context for language models in several ways: summary tokens the model learns
to write for itself \citep{mu2023gist,chevalier2023autocompressors}; autoencoders that produce memory
slots for a frozen decoder \citep{ge2024icae}; a single retrieval embedding projected into the decoder
\citep{cheng2024xrag}; context encoders read through cross-attention \citep{yen2024cepe}; and
dropping tokens, either independently of the question \citep{jiang2023llmlingua,pan2024llmlingua2}
or conditioned on it \citep{jiang2024longllmlingua}. Question-focused abstractive compression of
retrieved documents \citep{xu2024recomp} corresponds to our summary baseline. Vision--language models
connect separately trained encoders to language models through projections into the embedding space
\citep{alayrac2022flamingo,liu2023llava}. \bgkit{} combines a prompt-conditioned bidirectional encoder
with the decoder's embedding layer, applies it hierarchically to repositories and histories, and uses
it as the channel through which an agent receives all tool output.

\section{Conclusion}

\bgkit{} compresses text into vectors that a decoder reads through its embedding layer, under a prompt
that states what the reader needs. With a 350M encoder and a 350M decoder, a budget of a few thousand
positions gives the decoder access to contexts of tens to over a hundred thousand tokens. At equal
budget the compressed context scores above truncated text and token pruning on most of the benchmarks
we tested, and above BM25 retrieval where answers combine facts spread through the context, often with
a quarter of the positions; it loses exact strings, where retrieval does better. At the encoder's own
kept positions its vectors carry far more than the tokens there, and what they carry depends on the
prompt. The same interface lets an agent work with every tool result in compressed form, and keep a
repository overview or a memory index in context as standing background. Our evidence for that use
is limited to a 350M agent (Section~\ref{sec:agents}). The case for it grows with the decoder, since
the encoder's cost does not depend on the decoder's size; pairing a small encoder with a much larger
decoder is the natural next step. Next steps are
a decoder trained on text at the same budget as a matched baseline, encodings that can be reused across
questions, and combining retrieval with compression.
